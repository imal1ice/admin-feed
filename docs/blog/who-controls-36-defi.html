I read who controls 36 DeFi protocols. Here's the scoreboard.

In my last note I argued that the control plane — who can upgrade, mint, pause or move funds — is the attack surface almost nobody maps. Here's the evidence.

Between 14 and 26 October 2026 I read the admin setup of 36 mid-size DeFi protocols (roughly $10M–$400M TVL) on Ethereum, Arbitrum, Base and Optimism. For each I asked the same question: who can upgrade the contracts, mint the token, pause, move collateral or change the oracle — and does anything slow them down? Everything came from public chain state and verified source, read with ownerwatch. No protocol is named — several findings aren't fixed yet, and twenty teams got theirs privately first. This is the aggregate scoreboard.

The scoreboard

  • 10 of 36 put at least one privileged role on a single externally-owned key — one transaction, no second signature, no delay.
  • 16 of 36 rely on a Safe with no timelock on at least one admin path.
  • 8 of 36 were genuinely well run — every dangerous power behind a multisig and a real delay.
  • 10+ of 36 publish documentation that contradicts their own contracts.
  • 2 couldn't be verified from public information at all.

Three things that stood out

One key, one transaction. For those ten protocols, a single hot wallet — some with thousands of transactions on it — can upgrade the code, appoint a minter, blacklist holders or move collateral, alone and instantly. Some teams say the key sits behind MPC or a custodian. Maybe so — but nothing on-chain shows it, and a holder can't check it.

A multisig, but no clock. The most common shape wasn't a single key; it was a Safe whose code upgrade waits the advertised 24–48 hours, while the same Safe can change roles, mint limits, oracles or pause state instantly. Thresholds as low as 2-of-3 guarded tens of millions. The delay you were promised often guards only the one door nobody uses.

Supply that didn't come through the minter. One stablecoin's docs say every token is minted one-for-one against collateral through its minting contract. On-chain, most of the supply ever issued was minted directly by the owner, outside that contract — twice through a timelock whose delay was 60 seconds. The tokens may well be backed. The point is the chain doesn't show it, and the docs describe a mechanism most of the supply never used.

What to take from it

None of this is a hack, and none of it is an accusation — most are disclosed design choices. But two thirds of a representative slice of mid-size DeFi have at least one privileged path that is faster, or more concentrated, than their own documentation implies. “Trust the multisig” is carrying a lot of weight it often hasn't earned.
